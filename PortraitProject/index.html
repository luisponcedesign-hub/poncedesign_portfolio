Pioneers of Computing

Twelve people whose work laid the foundations of computers, software, and the internet as we know them.

Alan Turing

Laid the theoretical foundation for computing and AI with the Turing machine concept.

"A computer could be said to 'think' if a human interrogator could not tell it apart, through conversation, from a human being." — Alan Turing, on the concept behind the Turing Test
Photo: Wikimedia Commons, 1951

Ada Lovelace

Wrote the first algorithm intended for a machine — often called the first programmer.

"The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform." — Ada Lovelace, Notes on the Analytical Engine, 1843
Photo: Wikimedia Commons, c. 1840

Charles Babbage

Designed the Analytical Engine, an early mechanical general-purpose computer concept.

"We may say most aptly that the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves." — Ada Lovelace, describing Babbage's Analytical Engine
Portrait by Samuel Laurence — Wikimedia Commons

John von Neumann

Developed the stored-program architecture most modern computers still use.

"The importance of the computer in modern society lies not in what we can do with it, but in what we cannot do without it." — widely attributed to John von Neumann
Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Grace Hopper

Pioneered compilers and helped create COBOL, shaping how humans write code.

"It's much easier for most people to write an English statement than it is to use symbols. So I decided data processors ought to be able to write their programs in English, and the computers would translate them into machine code." — Grace Hopper, on the idea behind the compiler
U.S. Navy photo — Wikimedia Commons

Claude Shannon

Founded information theory, the math underlying digital communication.

"He's one of the great men of the century. Without him, none of the things we know today would exist. The whole digital revolution started with him." — Neil Sloane, mathematician, on Claude Shannon
Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Tim Berners-Lee

Invented the World Wide Web.

"I just had to take the hypertext idea and connect it to the TCP and DNS ideas and — ta-da! — the World Wide Web." — Tim Berners-Lee
Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Steve Wozniak

Engineered the Apple I and II, key to personal computing's rise.

"I never wanted to run a company. I just wanted to design good computers." — Steve Wozniak
Photo: Wikimedia Commons, 1983

Dennis Ritchie

Created the C programming language and co-created Unix.

"The tools that Dennis built — and their direct descendants — run pretty much everything today." — Brian Kernighan, on Dennis Ritchie
Photo: Wikimedia Commons, 2011

Linus Torvalds

Created Linux and Git, cornerstones of modern software infrastructure.

"I'm an egotistical bastard, and I name all my projects after myself. First 'Linux', now 'Git'." — Linus Torvalds
Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Margaret Hamilton

Led the Apollo onboard flight software team and coined the term "software engineering."

"When I first came up with the term, no one had heard of it before, at least in our world. It was an ongoing joke for a long time." — Margaret Hamilton, on coining "software engineering"
Photo: Daphne Weld Nichols, 1995 — Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Radia Perlman

Invented the spanning-tree protocol, which lets large networks route around loops and stay connected.

"I think that I shall never see a graph more lovely than a tree." — Radia Perlman, from "Algorhyme", her 1985 spanning-tree paper
Photo: Wikimedia Commons, 2009 — public domain

Portraits sourced from Wikimedia Commons (public domain / freely licensed). Quotes are attributed as noted above.
